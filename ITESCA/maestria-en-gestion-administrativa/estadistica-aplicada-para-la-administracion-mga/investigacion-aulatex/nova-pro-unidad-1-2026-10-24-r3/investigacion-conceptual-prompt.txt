[holmesMuestreo2023:L20] Data may come from a population or from a sample. Lowercase letters like
[holmesMuestreo2023:L21] x
[holmesMuestreo2023:L23] or
[holmesMuestreo2023:L24] y
[holmesMuestreo2023:L26] generally are used to represent data values. Most data can be put into the following categories:
[holmesMuestreo2023:L27] Qualitative
[holmesMuestreo2023:L28] Quantitative
[holmesMuestreo2023:L29] Qualitative data
[holmesMuestreo2023:L30] are the result of categorizing or describing attributes of a population.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L31] Qualitative data
[holmesMuestreo2023:L32] are also often called categorical data. Hair color, blood type, ethnic group, the car a person drives, and the street a person lives on are examples of qualitative (categorical) data. Qualitative (categorical) data are generally described by words or letters. For instance, hair color might be black, dark brown, light brown, blonde, gray, or red. Blood type might be AB+, O-, or B+. Researchers often prefer to use quantitative data over qualitative (categorical) data because it lends itself more easily to mathematical analysis. For example, it does not make sense to find an average hair color or blood type.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L33] Quantitative data
[holmesMuestreo2023:L34] are always numbers. Quantitative data are the result of
[holmesMuestreo2023:L35] counting
[holmesMuestreo2023:L36] or
[holmesMuestreo2023:L37] measuring
[holmesMuestreo2023:L38] attributes of a population. Amount of money, pulse rate, weight, number of people living in your town, and number of students who take statistics are examples of quantitative data. Quantitative data may be either
[holmesMuestreo2023:L39] discrete
[holmesMuestreo2023:L40] or
[holmesMuestreo2023:L41] continuous
[holmesMuestreo2023:L42] .
[holmesMuestreo2023:L43] All data that are the result of counting are called
[holmesMuestreo2023:L44] quantitative discrete data
[holmesMuestreo2023:L45] . These data take on only certain numerical values. If you count the number of phone calls you receive for each day of the week, you might get values such as zero, one, two, or three.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L46] Data that are not only made up of counting numbers, but that may include fractions, decimals, or irrational numbers, are called
[holmesMuestreo2023:L47] quantitative continuous data
[holmesMuestreo2023:L48] . Continuous data are often  the results of measurements like lengths, weights, or times. A list of the lengths in minutes for all the phone calls that you make in a week, with numbers like 2.4, 7.5, or 11.0, would be quantitative continuous data.

[holmesMuestreo2023:L246] Sampling
[holmesMuestreo2023:L247] Gathering information about an entire population often costs too much or is virtually impossible. Instead, we use a sample of the population.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L248] A sample should have the same characteristics as the population it is representing.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L249] Most statisticians use various methods of random sampling in an attempt to achieve this goal. This section will describe a few of the most common methods. There are several different methods of
[holmesMuestreo2023:L250] random sampling
[holmesMuestreo2023:L251] . In each form of random sampling, each member of a population initially has an equal chance of being selected for the sample. Each method has pros and cons. The easiest method to describe is called a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L252] simple random sample
[holmesMuestreo2023:L253] . Any group of
[holmesMuestreo2023:L254] n
[holmesMuestreo2023:L255] individuals is equally likely to be chosen as any other group of
[holmesMuestreo2023:L256] n
[holmesMuestreo2023:L257] individuals if the simple random sampling technique is used. In other words, each sample of the same size has an equal chance of being selected.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L258] Besides simple random sampling, there are other forms of sampling that involve a chance process for getting the sample.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L259] Other well-known random sampling methods are the stratified sample, the cluster sample, and the systematic sample.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L260] To choose a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L261] stratified sample
[holmesMuestreo2023:L262] , divide the population into groups called strata and then take a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L263] proportionate
[holmesMuestreo2023:L264] number from each stratum. For example, you could stratify (group) your college population by department and then choose a proportionate simple random sample from each stratum (each department) to get a stratified random sample. To choose a simple random sample from each department, number each member of the first department, number each member of the second department, and do the same for the remaining departments. Then use simple random sampling to choose proportionate numbers from the first department and do the same for each of the remaining departments. Those numbers picked from the first department, picked from the second department, and so on represent the members who make up the stratified sample.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L265] To choose a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L266] cluster sample
[holmesMuestreo2023:L267] , divide the population into clusters (groups) and then randomly select some of the clusters. All the members from these clusters are in the cluster sample. For example, if you randomly sample four departments from your college population, the four departments make up the cluster sample. Divide your college faculty by department. The departments are the clusters. Number each department, and then choose four different numbers using simple random sampling. All members of the four departments with those numbers are the cluster sample.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L268] To choose a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L269] systematic sample
[holmesMuestreo2023:L270] , randomly select a starting point and take every
[holmesMuestreo2023:L271] n
[holmesMuestreo2023:L272] th
[holmesMuestreo2023:L273] piece of data from a listing of the population. For example, suppose you have to do a phone survey. Your phone book contains 20,000 residence listings. You must choose 400 names for the sample. Number the population 1–20,000 and then use a simple random sample to pick a number that represents the first name in the sample. Then choose every fiftieth name thereafter until you have a total of 400 names (you might have to go back to the beginning of your phone list). Systematic sampling is frequently chosen because it is a simple method.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L274] A type of sampling that is non-random is convenience sampling.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L275] Convenience sampling
[holmesMuestreo2023:L276] involves using results that are readily available. For example, a computer software store conducts a marketing study by interviewing potential customers who happen to be in the store browsing through the available software. The results of convenience sampling may be very good in some cases and highly biased (favor certain outcomes) in others.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L277] Sampling data should be done very carefully. Collecting data carelessly can have devastating results. Surveys mailed to households and then returned may be very biased (they may favor a certain group). It is better for the person conducting the survey to select the sample respondents.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L278] True random sampling is done
[holmesMuestreo2023:L279] with replacement
[holmesMuestreo2023:L280] .  That is, once a member is picked, that member goes back into the population and thus may be chosen more than once. However for practical reasons, in most populations, simple random sampling is done
[holmesMuestreo2023:L281] without replacement
[holmesMuestreo2023:L282] . Surveys are typically done without replacement.  That is, a member of the population may be chosen only once.  Most samples are taken from large populations and the sample tends to be small in comparison to the population. Since this is the case,
[holmesMuestreo2023:L283] sampling without replacement
[holmesMuestreo2023:L284] is approximately the same as sampling with replacement because the chance of picking the same individual more than once with replacement is very low.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L285] Sampling without replacement instead of sampling with replacement becomes a mathematical issue only when the population is small.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L286] When you analyze data, it is important to be aware of
[holmesMuestreo2023:L287] sampling errors
[holmesMuestreo2023:L288] and nonsampling errors. The actual process of sampling causes sampling errors. For example, the sample may not be large enough. Factors not related to the sampling process cause
[holmesMuestreo2023:L289] nonsampling errors
[holmesMuestreo2023:L290] . A defective counting device can cause a nonsampling error.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L291] In reality, a sample will never be exactly representative of the population so there will always be some sampling error.  As a rule, the larger the sample, the smaller the sampling error.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L292] In statistics, a
[holmesMuestreo2023:L293] sampling bias
[holmesMuestreo2023:L294] is created when a sample is collected from a population and some members of the population are not as likely to be chosen as others (remember, each member of the population should have an equally likely chance of being chosen).  When a sampling bias happens, there can be incorrect conclusions drawn about the population that is being studied.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L295] Critical Evaluation
[holmesMuestreo2023:L296] We need to evaluate the statistical studies we read about critically and analyze them before accepting the results of the studies. Common problems to be aware of include
[holmesMuestreo2023:L297] Problems with samples: A sample must be representative of the population. A sample that is not representative of the population is biased. Biased samples that are not representative of the population give results that are inaccurate and not valid.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L298] Self-selected samples: Responses only by people who choose to respond, such as call-in
[holmesMuestreo2023:L299] surveys, are often unreliable.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L300] Sample size issues: Samples that are too small may be unreliable. Larger samples are better, if possible. In some situations, having small samples is unavoidable and can still be used to draw conclusions. Examples: crash testing cars or medical testing for rare conditions
[holmesMuestreo2023:L301] Undue influence:  collecting data or asking questions in a way that influences the response
[holmesMuestreo2023:L302] Non-response or refusal of subject to participate:  The collected responses may no longer be representative of the population.  Often, people with strong positive or negative opinions may answer surveys, which can affect the results.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L303] Causality: A relationship between two variables does not mean that one causes the other to occur. They may be related (correlated) because of their relationship through a different variable.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L304] Self-funded or self-interest studies: A study performed by a person or organization in order to support their claim. Is the study impartial? Read the study carefully to evaluate the work. Do not automatically assume that the study is good, but do not automatically assume the study is bad either. Evaluate it on its merits and the work done.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L305] Misleading use of data: improperly displayed graphs, incomplete data, or lack of context
[holmesMuestreo2023:L306] Confounding:  When the effects of multiple factors on a response cannot be separated.  Confounding makes it difficult or impossible to draw valid conclusions about the effect of each factor.

[holmesMuestreo2023:L414] 1.12
[holmesMuestreo2023:L415] Problem
[holmesMuestreo2023:L416] Determine the type of sampling used (simple random, stratified, systematic, cluster, or convenience).
[holmesMuestreo2023:L417] A soccer coach selects six players from a group of boys aged eight to ten, seven players from a group of boys aged 11 to 12, and three players from a group of boys aged 13 to 14 to form a recreational soccer team.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L418] A pollster interviews all human resource personnel in five different high tech companies.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L419] A high school educational researcher interviews 50 public high school teachers and 50 private high school teachers.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L420] A medical researcher interviews every third cancer patient from a list of cancer patients at a local hospital.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L421] A high school counselor uses a computer to generate 50 random numbers and then picks students whose names correspond to the numbers.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L422] A student interviews classmates in their algebra class to determine how many pairs of jeans a student owns, on the average.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L423] Solution
[holmesMuestreo2023:L424] a. stratified; b. cluster; c. stratified; d. systematic; e. simple random; f.convenience
[holmesMuestreo2023:L425] Try It
[holmesMuestreo2023:L426] 1.12
[holmesMuestreo2023:L427] Determine the type of sampling used (simple random, stratified, systematic, cluster, or convenience).
[holmesMuestreo2023:L428] A high school principal polls 50 first-year students, 50 sophomores, 50 juniors, and 50 seniors regarding policy changes for after school activities.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L429] If we were to examine two samples representing the same population, even if we used random sampling methods for the samples, they would not be exactly the same. Just as there is variation in data, there is variation in samples. As you become accustomed to sampling, the variability will begin to seem natural.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L430] NOTE
[holmesMuestreo2023:L431] In
[holmesMuestreo2023:L432] Confidence Intervals
[holmesMuestreo2023:L433] of the text, sample size formulas are provided which will determine sample sizes when sampling from a population. The sample size will be a function of the desired precision and not a function of the population size. It may be somewhat counterintuitive that the sample size does not depend on the population size. However, this implies that a sample size of 1,000 can be adequate to represent a population of 100,000 versus 1,000,000 given that the same level of precision is desired. When working in
[holmesMuestreo2023:L434] Confidence Intervals
[holmesMuestreo2023:L435] with sample size formulas, the student will notice that population size is not a factor in determining the sample size.

[holmesMuestreo2023:L538] Be aware that as you take data, your data may vary somewhat from the data someone else is taking for the same purpose. This is completely natural. However, if two or more of you are taking the same data and get very different results, it is time for you and the others to reevaluate your data-taking methods and your accuracy.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L539] Variation in Samples
[holmesMuestreo2023:L540] It was mentioned previously that two or more
[holmesMuestreo2023:L541] samples
[holmesMuestreo2023:L542] from the same
[holmesMuestreo2023:L543] population
[holmesMuestreo2023:L544] , taken randomly, and having close to the same characteristics of the population will likely be different from each other. Suppose Doreen and Jung both decide to study the average amount of time students at their college sleep each night.  Doreen and Jung each take samples of 500 students. Doreen uses systematic sampling and Jung uses cluster sampling. Doreen's sample will be different from Jung's sample. Even if Doreen and Jung used the same sampling method, in all likelihood their samples would be different. Neither would be wrong, however.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L545] Think about what contributes to making Doreen’s and Jung’s samples different.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L546] If Doreen and Jung took larger samples (i.e. the number of data values is increased), their sample results (the average amount of time a student sleeps) might be closer to the actual population average. But still, their samples would be, in all likelihood, different from each other. This
[holmesMuestreo2023:L547] variability in samples
[holmesMuestreo2023:L548] cannot be stressed enough.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L549] Size of a Sample
[holmesMuestreo2023:L550] The size of a sample (often called the number of observations, usually given the symbol n) is important. The examples you have seen in this book so far have been small. Samples of only a few hundred observations, or even smaller, are sufficient for many purposes. In polling, samples that are from 1,200 to 1,500 observations are considered large enough and good enough if the survey is random and is well done. Later we will find that even much smaller sample sizes will give very good results. You will learn why when you study confidence intervals.
[holmesMuestreo2023:L551] Be aware that many large samples are biased. For example, call-in surveys are invariably biased, because people choose to respond or not.

[holmesProporcion2023:L19] A Confidence Interval for A Population Proportion
[holmesProporcion2023:L20] During an election year, we see articles in the newspaper that state
[holmesProporcion2023:L21] confidence intervals
[holmesProporcion2023:L22] in terms of proportions or percentages. For example, a poll for a particular candidate running for president might show that the candidate has 40% of the vote within three percentage points  (if the sample is large enough). Often, election polls are calculated with 95% confidence, so, the pollsters would be 95% confident that the true proportion of voters who favored the candidate would be between 0.37 and 0.43.
[holmesProporcion2023:L23] Investors in the stock market are interested in the true proportion of stocks that go up and down each week. Businesses that sell personal computers are interested in the proportion of households in the United States that own personal computers. Confidence intervals can be calculated for the true proportion of stocks that go up or down each week and for the true proportion of households in the United States that own personal computers.
[holmesProporcion2023:L24] The procedure to find the confidence interval for a population proportion is similar to that for the population mean, but the formulas are a bit different although conceptually identical. While the formulas are different, they are based upon the same mathematical foundation given to us by the Central Limit Theorem. Because of this we will see the same basic format using the same three pieces of information: the sample value of the parameter in question, the standard deviation of the relevant sampling distribution, and the number of standard deviations we need to have the confidence in our estimate that we desire.

[nistTamano:L8] 7.2.4.2.
[nistTamano:L9] Sample sizes required
[nistTamano:L10] Derivation of formula for required sample
[nistTamano:L11] size when testing proportions
[nistTamano:L12] The method of determining sample sizes for testing proportions is similar
[nistTamano:L13] to the method for
[nistTamano:L14] determining sample sizes for
[nistTamano:L15] testing the mean
[nistTamano:L16] . Although the sampling distribution for proportions
[nistTamano:L17] actually follows a binomial distribution, the normal approximation
[nistTamano:L18] is used for this derivation.
[nistTamano:L19] Problem formulation
[nistTamano:L20] We want to test the hypothesis
[nistTamano:L21] \(H_0: \,\, p = p_0\)
[nistTamano:L22] \(H_a: \,\, p \ne p_0\)
[nistTamano:L23] with \(p\)
[nistTamano:L24] denoting the proportion of defectives.
[nistTamano:L25] Define \(\delta\)
[nistTamano:L26] as the change in the proportion defective that we are interested
[nistTamano:L27] in detecting
[nistTamano:L28] \(\delta = | p_1 - p_0|\).
[nistTamano:L29] Specify the level of statisitical significance and statistical
[nistTamano:L30] power, respectively, by
[nistTamano:L31] \(P(\mbox{reject } H_0 | H_0 \mbox{ is true with any } p \ne p_0) 
[nistTamano:L32]        \le \alpha\)
[nistTamano:L33] \(P(\mbox{reject } H_0 | H_0 \mbox{ is false with any } p \le \delta) 
[nistTamano:L34]        \le 1-\beta\).
[nistTamano:L35] Definition of allowable deviation
[nistTamano:L36] If we are interested in detecting a change in the proportion defective
[nistTamano:L37] of size \(\delta\)
[nistTamano:L38] in either direction, the corresponding confidence interval for \(p\)
[nistTamano:L39] can be written as
[nistTamano:L41] $$ \hat{p} - \delta \le p \le \hat{p} + \delta \, . $$
[nistTamano:L42] Relationship to confidence interval
[nistTamano:L43] For a \(100(1-\alpha)\) %
[nistTamano:L44] confidence interval based on the normal distribution, where \(z_{1 - \alpha/2}\)
[nistTamano:L45] is the
[nistTamano:L46] critical value of
[nistTamano:L47] the normal distribution
[nistTamano:L48] which is exceeded with probability \(\alpha/2\),
[nistTamano:L49] is
[nistTamano:L51] $$ \delta = z_{1-\alpha/2} \, \sqrt{\frac{p_0 (1-p_0)}{N}} + z_{1-\beta} \,
[nistTamano:L52]    \sqrt{\frac{p_1 (1-p_1)}{N}} \, . $$
[nistTamano:L53] Minimum sample size
[nistTamano:L54] Thus, the minimum sample size is
[nistTamano:L55] For a two-sided interval
[nistTamano:L56] $$ N \ge  \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} \, 
[nistTamano:L57]        \sqrt{p_0 (1-p_0)} + z_{1-\beta} \, 
[nistTamano:L58]        \sqrt{p_1 (1-p_1)}}{\delta} \, \right)^2 \, . $$
[nistTamano:L59] For a one-sided interval
[nistTamano:L60] $$ N \ge  \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha} \, \sqrt{p_0 (1-p_0)} + z_{1-\beta} 
[nistTamano:L61]          \, \sqrt{p_1 (1-p_1)}}{\delta} \, \right)^2 \, . $$
[nistTamano:L62] The mathematical details of this derivation are given on pages
[nistTamano:L63]     30-34 of
[nistTamano:L64] Fleiss, Levin, and Paik
[nistTamano:L65] .
[nistTamano:L66] Continuity correction
[nistTamano:L67] Fleiss, Levin, and Paik also recommend the following continuity
[nistTamano:L68] correction,
[nistTamano:L69] \( N = N' + 1/\delta \, , \)
[nistTamano:L70] with \(N'\)
[nistTamano:L71] denoting the sample size computed using the above formula.
[nistTamano:L72] Example of calculating sample size for testing
[nistTamano:L73] proportion defective
[nistTamano:L74] Suppose that a department manager needs to be able to detect any
[nistTamano:L75] change above 0.10 in the current proportion defective of his product
[nistTamano:L76] line, which is running at approximately 10 % defective. He is interested
[nistTamano:L77] in a one-sided test and does not want to stop the line except
[nistTamano:L78] when the process has clearly degraded and, therefore, he chooses a
[nistTamano:L79] significance level for the test of 5 %. Suppose, also, that he is
[nistTamano:L80] willing to take a risk of 10 % of failing to detect a change of this
[nistTamano:L81] magnitude.  With these criteria:
[nistTamano:L82] \(z_{0.95} = 1.645 , \,\, z_{0.90} = 1.282\)
[nistTamano:L83] \(\delta = 0.10 \,\, (p_1 = 0.20)\)
[nistTamano:L84] \(p_0 = 0.10\)
[nistTamano:L85] and the minimum sample size for a
[nistTamano:L86] one-sided test
[nistTamano:L87] procedure
[nistTamano:L88] is
[nistTamano:L90] $$\begin{eqnarray}
[nistTamano:L91] N & \ge & \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha} \, \sqrt{p_0 (1-p_0)} + z_{1-\beta} \, \sqrt{p_1 (1-p_1)}}{\delta} \, \right)^2 \\
[nistTamano:L92]    &  & \\
[nistTamano:L93]    &   =    & \left( \frac{1.645 \, \sqrt{0.1 \times 0.9} + 1.282 \, \sqrt{0.2 \times 0.8}}{0.1} \, \right)^2 \\
[nistTamano:L94]    &  & \\
[nistTamano:L96]    & \approx & 102 \, . 
[nistTamano:L97] \end{eqnarray} $$
[nistTamano:L98] With the continuity correction, the minimum sample size becomes 112.
